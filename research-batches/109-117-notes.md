# Research decisions: #109–#117

Reviewed 2026-07-30.

This batch covers Koffing through Seadra. It was researched in four coherent groups: Koffing/Weezing, Rhyhorn/Rhydon, Chansey/Tangela/Kangaskhan, and Horsea/Seadra.

## Source approach

The normal base source set is supplemented with:

- French-localizer commentary preserved by Poképédia for every French name in the batch and for several Japanese constructions;
- Kotobank dictionary entries for Japanese `毒ガス`, `ラッキー`, `もじゃもじゃ`, `毛むくじゃら`, and `竜の落し子`;
- CNRTL entries for relevant French vocabulary;
- Merriam-Webster entries for English respiratory, luck, tangle, and ruler vocabulary.

Localizer commentary is used as confirmation when it explicitly identifies a construction. Dictionary sources establish meaning, register, and borrowing status but are not treated as proof of naming intent by themselves.

## #109 Koffing / Smogo / Dogars

- Japanese is analyzed as contracted `毒ガス` (*doku-gasu*, poison gas).
- `ガス` is tagged as a loanword. Japanese dictionaries record both Dutch and English transmission, while the naming commentary specifically treats the component as English `gas`; the entry explains both facts without pretending the wider lexical history has only one route.
- French `Smogo` is confirmed from `smog`; the final `-o` is treated as a mascot-like reshaping rather than an invented second root.
- English is grounded in `coughing`, with the altered spelling also functioning as a written cough sound.

## #110 Weezing / Smogogo / Matadogas

- Japanese `又` (*mata*, again/another) + `毒ガス` is confirmed from naming commentary and matches the visible second gas sphere.
- French combines `smog` with `gogo / à gogo`; the localizer also explained that repeating `-go` encodes two Smogo.
- English advances the respiratory progression from coughing to wheezing.
- Japanese `ガス` and French `smog` receive entry-owned loanword tags.

## #111 Rhyhorn / Rhinocorne / Sihorn

- Japanese `犀` (*sai*, rhinoceros) + English-derived `ホーン` is confirmed from naming commentary.
- French `rhinocéros + corne` is confirmed.
- English `rhino + horn` is linguistically transparent and marked strong rather than confirmed because no English creator statement was located.
- Only the Japanese `ホーン` component receives a loanword tag.

## #112 Rhydon / Rhinoféros / Sidon

- Japanese `犀 + -ドン` is confirmed from commentary identifying `-don` as a giant-monster ending associated with Japanese fiction, including kaiju and sentai enemies.
- French `rhinocéros + féroce` is confirmed.
- English `Rhydon` remains plausible: Spanish `don` (lord) and the broader creature-name or prehistoric `-don` sound are both reasonable. The entry deliberately does not collapse them into one claimed fact.

## #113 Chansey / Leveinard / Lucky

- Japanese `ラッキー` is an established English loanword and receives an entry-owned tag.
- French `le + veinard` is confirmed and explained as the compact phrase “the lucky one.”
- English combines `chance` with a name-like `-y`, producing a spelling close to `chancy`.
- Notes preserve the useful tension that modern `chancy` usually means risky or uncertain, while older/limited usage can mean lucky; Chansey clearly foregrounds favorable chance.

## #114 Tangela / Saquedeneu / Monjara

This is the batch’s principal unresolved case.

- Poképédia relays French-localizer commentary identifying Japanese `Monjara` with `monjayaki`.
- Specialist Pokémon references instead emphasize `もじゃもじゃ` (*mojamoja*, shaggy/tangled), `毛むくじゃら` (*kemukujara*, densely hairy), and sometimes `monster`.
- The food and shaggy readings overlap phonetically and can coexist as associations. The entry gives the reported *monjayaki* account priority in Roots but retains the exceptionally natural shaggy/tangle vocabulary in the same claim and Notes.
- The confidence remains strong rather than confirmed because the Japanese naming intent is being relayed through a non-Japanese localizer source and competing specialist traditions remain meaningful.
- French `sac de nœuds` is confirmed.
- English `tangle + -ela/-ella` remains plausible. Tangela’s documented beta name `Meduza` establishes that Medusa imagery was considered during development, but does not prove that `Medusa` is a root of the final name.

## #115 Kangaskhan / Kangourex / Garura

- Japanese probably compresses `カンガルー` (*kangaroo*) + `ルーラー` (*ruler*), but the seam is too compressed to tag either component confidently. The reading remains plausible.
- French `kangourou + rex` is confirmed; `rex` is explained as Latin “king.”
- English `kangaroo + khan` is strong. `Khan` is explained as a ruler title; Genghis Khan is a familiar association but is not required as the unique intended person.

## #116 Horsea / Hypotrempe / Tattu

- Japanese is confirmed as a shortening of `タツノオトシゴ`, the ordinary word for seahorse.
- Notes explain the underlying `竜の落とし子` expression and its dragon/offspring imagery without treating the literal phrase as a separate portmanteau.
- French `hippocampe + tremper` is confirmed.
- English reverses and compresses `sea horse`; `horsie` is retained only as a secondary sound association.

## #117 Seadra / Hypocéan / Seadra

- Japanese and English `Seadra` are confirmed as `sea + dragon` from naming commentary.
- Japanese Roots display `シー` and clipped `ドラ`, and both receive separate English-loanword tags.
- French `hippocampe + océan` is confirmed and creates a clear scale progression from Hypotrempe’s immersion verb to the whole ocean.
