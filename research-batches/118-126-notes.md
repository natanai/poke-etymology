# Research decisions: #118–#126

Reviewed 2026-07-30.

This batch covers Goldeen through Magmar. It was researched in five coherent groups: the ornamental-goldfish pair, the Staryu family, Mr. Mime/Scyther/Jynx, and the paired standalone Electric and Fire species Electabuzz/Magmar.

## Source approach

The base source set is supplemented with:

- French-localizer explanations preserved by Poképédia;
- Japanese dictionary and cultural references for specific goldfish breeds and ordinary animal vocabulary;
- French dictionaries for historical spelling, register, and Occitan borrowing;
- English dictionaries for ordinary roots;
- specialist references only where they preserve unresolved alternatives that stronger evidence does not settle.

A localizer explanation confirms a construction only where it explicitly addresses that name. Dictionary evidence establishes lexical meaning or register but is not treated as proof of naming intent.

## #118 Goldeen / Poissirène / Tosakinto

- Japanese is grounded in `土佐金 / 土佐錦魚`, the Tosakin ornamental goldfish associated with Tosa/Kochi.
- The final `ト` is treated as creature-name reshaping rather than assigned an unsupported lexical meaning.
- French `poisson + sirène` follows documented localizer commentary.
- English `goldfish / golden + queen` is strong but not creator-confirmed.

## #119 Seaking / Poissoroy / Azumao

- Japanese `東錦` names another ornamental goldfish variety and combines with `王` (king).
- French uses `poisson + roy`, the historical spelling of modern `roi`.
- English is the transparent `sea + king`.

## #120 Staryu / Stari / Hitodeman

- Japanese combines ordinary `ヒトデ` (starfish) with borrowed English `マン`.
- French combines borrowed English `star` with the localizer's cute diminutive `-i`.
- English `star + you` establishes the family conversation later answered by Starmie.
- Japanese `マン` and French `star` receive entry-owned loanword tags.
- Ultraman is retained only as a recognizable superhero-name association, not a proven direct tribute.

## #121 Starmie / Staross / Starmie

- Japanese securely contains borrowed English `スター`; `ミー` probably represents English `me`, especially beside Staryu, while `mystery` remains a secondary specialist tradition.
- Only the secure `スター` component receives a Japanese tag.
- French `star + boss` is documented by the localizer; both borrowed English components receive tags.
- English `star + me` is strong through the direct family pairing.

## #122 Mr. Mime / M. Mime / Barrierd

- Japanese `barrier + weird` follows documented localizer commentary.
- The exact displayed borrowed token `バリヤー` receives a tag.
- French and English independently use their ordinary abbreviation for Mister/Monsieur plus `mime`.
- Notes identify the later species-sex mismatch as historical context, not an etymological root.

## #123 Scyther / Insécateur / Strike

- Japanese is the English borrowing `strike` and receives an exact-token tag.
- French `insecte + sécateur` is localizer-confirmed.
- English `scythe + -er` is transparent and strong.

## #124 Jynx / Lippoutou / Rougela

- Japanese securely begins with French-derived `ルージュ`, familiar in cosmetics vocabulary; its final `-ラ` and a possible reversed `la rouge` reading remain unresolved.
- French `lippu + poutou` follows localizer commentary.
- `poutou` is documented as an Occitan borrowing in French; both it and Japanese `ルージュ` receive tags with their donor languages.
- English is a deliberate fantasy respelling of `jinx`.
- The language-tag validator now recognizes `Occitan` as a documented donor language.

## #125 Electabuzz / Élektek / Eleboo

- Japanese probably combines clipped English-derived `エレ` from `electric` with the buzzing mimetic family `ブンブン`; only the secure clipped borrowed component is tagged.
- French `électrique + tekken` remains plausible because only a short `tek` boundary survives.
- English `electric + buzz` is strong.
- No French tag is attached to `tek`: it is a proposed one-off source element, not a secure French loanword token.

## #126 Magmar / Magmar / Boober

- French retains the international English name.
- `magma + mar` is strong for French and English, but not upgraded to confirmed creator intent.
- Japanese remains an unresolved sound cluster involving `booby`, `boost`, and `burn` associations.
- No Japanese tag is attached because the exact lexical boundaries and donor path are not secure.

## Naming-credit feature introduced with this batch

The same pull request introduces a documented naming-credit line inside every Japanese, French, and English disclosure, including pending entries.

The system distinguishes:

- a specific contributor to an exact name or family;
- a language-set creator or localizer;
- a naming-program lead;
- a credited team where no individual mapping survives;
- genuinely unknown attribution.

Generation I defaults are deliberately asymmetrical because the records are different:

- Japanese shows Game Freak's original naming staff and states that the individual species-name creator is not publicly documented.
- French shows the Nintendo France localization team. A contemporary report names Jean-Baptiste Fleury, Daniel Charbit, Nicolas Robert, Nicolas Gourio, and Pokémon specialist Julien Bardakoff; later interviews frequently summarize Bardakoff as the creator of the first 251 names. The site preserves both rather than claiming uncontested sole authorship for every species.
- English shows Hiro Nakamura as naming lead while stating that the exact coiner usually remains undocumented.

Species-specific English overrides currently document Gail Tilden for the Poliwag family, Nob Ogasawara's recommendation to retain Gyarados, and Bill Giese for the Articuno/Zapdos/Moltres pattern.

Credits are provenance data, not etymology evidence. They do not alter a Roots confidence label and are validated separately from language-analysis tags.
