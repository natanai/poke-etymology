# Audit notes: #082–#090

Reviewed 2026-07-30 under `RESEARCH_METHOD.md`.

- **Rarecoil / Magneton / Magnéton:** Japanese securely combines *rare* and *coil*, but the narrower proposal that *rare* specifically means a rare-earth magnet remains secondary. French and English use *magnéton / magneton*, an existing physics term, rather than merely an invented `magnet + -on` blend.
- **Kamonegi:** the Japanese name is not only `duck + scallion`; it invokes the proverb `鴨が葱を背負って来る`, in which a duck arrives carrying the onion needed to cook it and thereby represents an exceptionally convenient opportunity or easy target.
- **Canarticho:** Julien Bardakoff’s explanation supports `canard + artichaut` as an intentionally absurd substitution. The secondary French sense of *canard* as a false report is noted only as an incidental resonance, not promoted to an intended root.
- **Farfetch’d:** English preserves the ordinary adjective *far-fetched* almost intact. The name translates the proverb’s broader unbelievable or too-convenient quality rather than its duck-and-onion ingredients.
- **Dodo family:** Japanese Dodo is the animal word alone. Dodorio allows `鳥` (*tori*, bird) and `トリオ` (*torio*, trio) to overlap, while French and English transparently advance from duo to trio.
- **Pawou / Otaria / Seel:** Japanese is sound-symbolic, French uses the zoological word *otarie* plus Bardakoff’s described poetic ending, and English is a near-literal respelling. The design’s imperfect match to a real seal does not weaken the transparent name constructions.
- **Jugon / Lamantine / Dewgong:** Japanese is the zoological dugong term. Bardakoff’s French explanation supports `lamantin + comptine`; English securely combines *dew* and *dugong*. Notes distinguish dugongs and manatees from the Pokémon’s more seal-like visual design.
- **Betbeter / Tadmorv / Grimer:** Japanese uses common sticky mimetic forms. French is the documented phrase `tas de morve`. The unrelated *beto-beto-san* yōkai theory is omitted because it is unnecessary and weakly supported.
- **Betbeton:** `べとべと` remains secure, but the final `-ton` is unresolved. It may evoke weight or simply enlarge the family name; it is not presented as a standard Japanese suffix with a definite meaning.
- **Grotadmorv / Muk:** French explicitly enlarges the pile-of-snot joke with *gros*. English is treated as a respelling of *muck*, without inventing extra roots.
- **Shellder / Kokiyas:** Japanese and English share `shell + shelter`. French is a playful phonetic reshaping of *coquillage*, supported by the French-localizer source, rather than a forced multi-root portmanteau.

Every entry includes separate Japanese, French, and English analyses, Notes, a localization comparison, local confidence, review metadata, and visible sources. Repeated fan theories were retained only when stronger linguistic or primary evidence supported them.
