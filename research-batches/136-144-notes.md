# Research notes — #136–#144

Reviewed: 2026-07-30

This batch audits Flareon through Articuno in Japanese, French, and English. It preserves exact creator/localizer evidence where available and leaves unresolved seams visible rather than forcing every name into a clean portmanteau.

## #136 Flareon

- Japanese ブースター is the ordinary English loanword *booster*. The whole displayed token is tagged.
- French Pyroli continues Julien Bardakoff's coordinated Évoli/Aquali/Voltali/Pyroli system: a fire element plus shared `-li`, which he associated with Greek *lithos* and the evolutionary stones.
- English Flareon is transparently *flare* plus the shared family ending `-eon`.
- Pyroli already had a supported Bardakoff attribution override before this etymology audit; attribution and audit status remain separate.

## #137 Porygon

- Japanese ポリゴン is the established borrowing of English *polygon* and receives a whole-token tag.
- The r-series consonant is ordinary Japanese adaptation of a language that does not preserve the English /l/–/r/ contrast. Avoid caricaturing this as Japanese speakers simply “replacing L with R.”
- French and English retain the Japanese proper-name form. They do not receive tags merely because the retained international name ultimately began as a borrowing in Japanese.

## #138 Omanyte

- Omnite naturally overlaps Japanese オウムガイ, *nautilus*, and *ammonite*.
- The evidence supports the coiled-shell fossil field strongly but does not prove one perfectly segmented blend.
- French Amonita and English Omanyte are phonetic/name-like reshaping of *ammonite*.
- No loanword tag is used: the Japanese boundary is blended, while the fossil terminology is scientific rather than the ordinary lexical borrowing category this UI marks.

## #139 Omastar

- Japanese and French both add English *star* to the ammonite-family base.
- Japanese スター and French `star` receive exact entry-owned tags.
- The star reference describes the five-rayed visible body and may also elevate the evolution as the “star” of its line. It does not classify Omastar as a starfish.

## #140 Kabuto

- 兜 means helmet; 兜蟹 is the established Japanese name for a horseshoe crab.
- All three languages retain the Japanese proper name.
- The design can evoke both a helmet and horseshoe-crab shell without requiring Kabuto to be a literal modern horseshoe crab.

## #141 Kabutops

- `Kabuto` remains secure.
- The final element has competing specialist readings: Greek-derived scientific `-ops`, *Triops*, and an overlapping *tops* sound.
- None has enough direct evidence to replace the others, so all three language analyses remain plausible and preserve the uncertainty.
- Scientific/learned possibilities are not loanword-tagged.

## #142 Aerodactyl

- Japanese Ptera and French Ptéra abbreviate the prehistoric `pter-` vocabulary associated with pterosaurs/Pteranodon.
- English is *aero-* plus *pterodactyl*.
- These are learned Greek/scientific roots and are deliberately not shown with the ordinary loanword annotation.

## #143 Snorlax

- Junichi Masuda confirms that Kōji Nishino inspired the character's eating/sleeping behavior and that Game Freak staff nicknamed Nishino “Kirby.” Kabigon reshapes that nickname with a monster-like `-gon` ending.
- This inspiration does not identify Nishino as the coiner of the Japanese name.
- French Ronflex follows Bardakoff's *ronflement + flexible* explanation; common *ronfler + relax* associations reinforce the effect but do not replace the localizer's account.
- English is visibly rooted in *snore* plus *lax/relax*, but historical accounts differ around the exact second association and the additional *Esquilax* trigger.
- A published account attributes the English name suggestion to Bill Giese. A narrow English naming-credit override records that attribution while honestly labeling the source as a published account rather than a direct quotation.

## #144 Articuno

- Japanese フリーザー is the English loanword *freezer* and receives a whole-token tag.
- French Artikodin is *arctique + Odin*. Bardakoff specifically connected Odin with the Odin armor in *Saint Seiya*, which reminded him of the design; a narrow French credit override records that first-person naming explanation.
- English Articuno is *arctic + uno*. Bill Giese directly recalls designing the legendary-bird `uno/dos/tres` sequence.
- English `uno` receives a Spanish loanword tag. The system does not tag English material inside English analyses, but a securely imported Spanish numeral is a genuine cross-language component.
